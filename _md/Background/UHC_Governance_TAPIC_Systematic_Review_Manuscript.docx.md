**Running title:** TAPIC governance in UHC systems

# **Abstract**

**Background.** Achieving universal health coverage (UHC) depends not only on financing and benefit design but also on the quality of health-system governance. Evidence on how governance functions across the core functions of UHC systems has not been systematically synthesised using a common analytic framework.

**Objective.** To review and synthesise international evidence on the governance of UHC systems across core UHC functions using the TAPIC framework (Transparency, Accountability, Participation, Integrity and Policy Capacity), and to derive a set of indicators for assessing UHC governance.

**Methods.** We conducted a systematic review following PRISMA 2020. PubMed, EBSCOhost, Embase, Scopus, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, medRxiv and Google Scholar were searched from inception to 19 February 2026. Empirical studies and analytic reviews were eligible if they assessed governance in at least one core UHC function and analysed at least one TAPIC dimension. Records were screened in Covidence by at least two reviewers, with a third resolving disagreements. Findings were synthesised narratively by UHC function and TAPIC dimension.

**Results.** Of 626 records identified, 467 remained after de-duplication, 61 full texts were assessed and 44 studies were included. Most were qualitative (29; 65.9%); 19 were multi-country, regional or global studies, and Nigeria (5) and Thailand (3) were the most frequently studied single countries. Governing bodies and stewardship was the most frequently studied UHC function (28 studies; 63.6%) and provider contracting the least (10; 22.7%). Evidence was unevenly distributed across TAPIC: Policy Capacity was assessed in 37 studies (84.1%), Accountability in 30 (68.2%), Transparency in 20 (45.5%), Participation in 9 (20.5%) and Integrity in 3 (6.8%). The synthesis yielded 56 governance indicators (Transparency 12, Accountability 13, Participation 10, Integrity 9, Capacity 12). Recurrent challenges included undisclosed benefit packages and performance data, weak sanctions and political interference, exclusion of providers and vulnerable groups from decision-making, conflicts of interest and claim falsification, and shortages of technical, analytic and information-system capacity. Reported good practices included public reporting of performance and budgets, institutionalised complaint mechanisms and independent oversight, systematic citizen and stakeholder engagement, electronic claims with risk-based audit, and mature health technology assessment and purchasing capability.

**Conclusions.** Evidence on UHC governance is dominated by qualitative, descriptive studies concentrated on capacity and accountability, with thin evidence on integrity and participation and on purchasing functions such as contracting. The indicator set offers a practical basis for governance assessment, but empirical validation and quantitative evaluation linking governance to UHC outcomes are needed.

**Keywords:** universal health coverage; health system governance; TAPIC; transparency; accountability; participation; integrity; policy capacity; systematic review

# **1. Introduction**

Universal health coverage (UHC) means that all people can obtain the health services they need without suffering financial hardship, and it is a core target of the Sustainable Development Goals. Progress towards UHC has often been framed in terms of resources and entitlements: how much money is raised and pooled, which services are covered, and how providers are paid [1,2]. However, whether a system converts resources and entitlements into equitable, efficient and sustainable coverage also depends on governance, the rules, institutions and processes through which decisions are made, resources are managed and actors are held to account [3,4].

Over recent decades many countries have reformed their health insurance and financing arrangements to widen coverage, reduce out-of-pocket payments and improve quality, yet outcomes differ markedly. Systems that perform well tend to combine open information, clear responsibility, meaningful engagement of citizens and stakeholders, safeguards against conflicts of interest and corruption, and sufficient institutional capability to turn policy into practice. These attributes correspond to the TAPIC framework proposed by Greer and colleagues, which characterises governance through five dimensions: Transparency, Accountability, Participation, Integrity and Policy Capacity [5]. Related work on transparency, accountability and anti-corruption in health has likewise emphasised that these dimensions are interdependent [6].

Thailand is widely recognised as a successful example of UHC [7]. Nevertheless, governance challenges remain, including differences in governance mechanisms between the major health schemes, variation in citizen participation across governance dimensions, the management of conflicts of interest, and uneven institutional capacity across agencies. Lessons from countries with different contexts and levels of development can inform how such challenges are addressed.

Existing reviews of health-system governance tend to define the concept or catalogue tools, and few apply a single framework across the operational functions of a UHC system (governing bodies, revenue raising, budget allocation, benefit-package design, provider contracting, provider payment, and monitoring of quality and performance). This review therefore addresses the question: **what is the existing evidence on governance functions in UHC systems, assessed using the TAPIC framework across core UHC functions?** Its objectives were to (i) review and synthesise international evidence on UHC governance; (ii) analyse strengths, barriers and success factors within each TAPIC dimension; (iii) identify measurable indicators for assessing UHC governance; and (iv) identify evidence gaps that can guide policy and research.

# **2. Methods**

## **2.1 Design and reporting**

We conducted a systematic review of the literature and reported it in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement [8]. The review formed the first phase of a broader mixed-methods project on UHC governance, whose purposive country case studies are reported separately. ## **2.2 Review question and eligibility criteria**

The question was structured using the Population–Exposure–Outcome (PEO) format. The **population** comprised countries and health systems of any income level that were implementing, or developing, UHC. The **exposure** was governance arrangements and mechanisms, operationalised through the five TAPIC dimensions. The **outcomes** were governance-related outcomes such as public trust, efficiency in resource use, reduced corruption, stakeholder participation, quality of policy decision-making and equity.

Studies were eligible if they met all of the following criteria:

- They were conducted in, or concerned, a country implementing or developing UHC, and examined governance at national, subnational or health-fund level.
- They assessed governance in at least one core UHC function: governing bodies and stewardship; revenue raising; budget allocation; benefit-package design and decision-making; provider contracting; provider payment mechanisms and rates; or monitoring of quality and performance.
- They explicitly assessed or analysed governance in their methods, results or analytic framework.
- They analysed at least one TAPIC dimension (Transparency, Accountability, Participation, Integrity or Policy Capacity).
- They used a qualitative, quantitative or mixed-methods design, policy analysis, comparative case study, systematic review, or narrative review with an explicit analytic framework.

Studies were excluded if they were unrelated to UHC, health financing or health-system governance; addressed only health, service-delivery or financial outcomes without analysing governance mechanisms; were purely conceptual or opinion pieces without empirical evidence or case material (editorials, commentaries, letters, conference abstracts, news or promotional items); mentioned governance only superficially in the introduction, discussion or recommendations; addressed none of the TAPIC dimensions; or were duplicates or had no accessible full text.

We searched PubMed, EBSCOhost, Embase, Scopus and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and searched medRxiv and Google Scholar for grey and pre-print literature. # **3. Results**

## **3.1 Study selection**

The database searches identified 625 records: PubMed (217), EBSCOhost (135), Embase (96), medRxiv (92), Google Scholar (47), Scopus (24) and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (14). Citation searching added one record, giving 626 in total. After removal of 159 duplicates (154 identified by Covidence and 5 manually), 467 records were screened by title and abstract and 403 were excluded. Of the 64 reports sought for retrieval, 3 could not be retrieved, and 61 full texts were assessed for eligibility. Seventeen were excluded: 14 did not assess governance, 2 were not about UHC and 1 had an ineligible study design. Forty-four studies were included in the review (Figure 1). No studies were awaiting classification or ongoing.

## **3.5 Indicators for assessing UHC governance**

Synthesis of the included studies produced 56 indicators for assessing UHC governance: 12 for Transparency, 13 for Accountability, 10 for Participation, 9 for Integrity and 12 for Capacity (Table 4). The indicators cover structures, mechanisms, processes and performance rather than quantitative measures alone, and can be applied to different UHC functions.

**Transparency** indicators assess whether information, decision-making processes and performance can be accessed and understood. They span public availability of policies and strategies, openness of decision-making and policy dialogue, disclosure of financing and expenditure (including standardised reporting under the System of Health Accounts or National Health Accounts), transparency of resource-allocation criteria, benefit entitlements, benefit-package decisions, provider selection, contracting and payment, audited financial reporting, public access to entitlement information, and public reporting of provider and system performance.

**Accountability** indicators assess whether decision-makers answer for their decisions and whether action follows non-compliance. They include clear roles and responsibilities, institutional and legislative oversight, financial accountability and audit, accountability in priority-setting, provider performance, accreditation, licensing and contracting, claims verification, corrective action, complaint and redress mechanisms, social accountability (community scorecards, social audits, citizen monitoring), enforceable sanctions, and follow-through on policy implementation.

**Participation** indicators assess who participates, at what stage, and whether participation can influence decisions. They include stakeholder, citizen, community and civil-society participation; representation on governing boards and committees; participation in benefit-package priority-setting, provider–purchaser negotiation and monitoring; formal feedback and consultation channels; multisectoral coordination; and, importantly, the inclusion of marginalised and diverse populations, which requires assessing whether disadvantaged groups can participate in practice and not only whether consultation exists.

**Integrity** indicators assess safeguards against corruption, conflicts of interest, misuse of power and unfair treatment. They include anti-corruption mechanisms, prevention and detection of provider claim fraud, prevention of diversion of health funds, control of bribery and informal payments, conflict-of-interest management, objective and fair decision-making, independent verification and audit, enforcement of ethical and regulatory standards, and equitable, impartial treatment.

**Capacity** indicators assess whether institutions have the resources, skills, systems and coordination ability to perform governance functions and implement decisions: adequacy of human resources and technical expertise, leadership and management, institutional capacity, health information and digital systems, analytic and evidence-use capacity, health technology assessment (HTA) and priority-setting, financial management and revenue raising, strategic purchasing and provider payment, regulation and enforcement, monitoring and evaluation, stakeholder and civil-society capacity, and coordination and implementation capacity.

## **3.6 Challenges and good practices by TAPIC dimension**

Table 5 summarises recurrent challenges and reported good practices in each dimension. Country names indicate where a challenge or practice was described in the included evidence; challenges were reported mostly in low- and middle-income settings, and good practices were mostly drawn from high-performing systems.

### ***Transparency***

Common challenges were top-down decision-making without public communication (for example, Nigeria), monitoring reports and quality data withheld from the public (Kenya), benefit-package designs that remain undisclosed (Ghana, Tanzania), and fragmented tracking of health expenditure with private-sector data largely absent. Reported good practices included public reporting on performance and budgets, common in high-performing systems; robust stakeholder consultation and public-disclosure mechanisms (Australia, Thailand); and transparent HTA processes informing decisions.

### ***Accountability***

Challenges included token sanctions and weak provider oversight (Nigeria), political interference through diversion of funds and appointment of officials (Ghana), absent subnational accountability frameworks leading to fund leakage (Nigeria), and under-resourced claims vetting that permits fraud and over-billing. Good practices included institutionalised complaint mechanisms and strong oversight in high-performing systems, national performance agreements between federal and state governments (Australia), decentralised governance with strong local accountability (Nordic countries), and electronic claims, risk-based audits and independent purchasing.

### ***Participation***

Challenges included the exclusion of providers and beneficiaries from benefit-package design (Nigeria), a gap between policymakers and frontline staff during reform implementation (Ireland), exclusion of vulnerable groups and rural populations from scheme governance (Ghana), and social accountability mechanisms with limited demonstrated impact on health outcomes (South and South-East Asia). Good practices included well-established citizen and stakeholder engagement within governance structures of high-performing systems, successful engagement of Indigenous communities in primary health care planning (Australia), systematic inclusion of citizens in health planning and benefit-package decisions, and continuous collaboration among policymakers, providers and communities.

### ***Integrity***

Challenges included unresolved conflicts of interest in governing councils (Nigeria), market concentration and distorted incentives (Georgia), widespread informal payments, absenteeism and claim falsification in low- and middle-income countries, and diversion of funds by political actors for non-health purposes (Ghana). Good practices included strong legislative frameworks to prevent conflicts of interest, mandatory electronic claims with biometric verification to reduce fraud, independent anti-corruption investigations with authority to act, and enforceable sanctions with robust oversight.

### ***Policy Capacity***

Challenges included a lack of technical staff for strategic purchasing and HTA (Nigeria, Kenya), fragmented health information systems that prevent real-time monitoring and data use, “implementation fatigue” and absent change-management expertise (Ireland), and weak subnational financial management that hinders budget absorption (India, Malawi). Good practices included strong institutional, technical and analytic capacity in high-performing countries, well-developed HTA systems, mature strategic-purchasing capability, and integrated health information systems supporting both policy and operations.

# **4. Discussion**

## **4.1 Principal findings**

This systematic review of 44 studies examined UHC governance through the TAPIC framework across the core functions of UHC systems. Three findings stand out. First, the evidence base is largely qualitative and descriptive, and is concentrated on institutional structures and stakeholder experience rather than measured effects. Second, evidence is distributed unevenly across TAPIC: Policy Capacity and Accountability are well represented, whereas Participation and Integrity are thinly covered. Third, across functions, the literature emphasises governing bodies, financing and performance monitoring, and gives less attention to purchasing functions, especially provider contracting.

The thin coverage of integrity and participation should be interpreted with care. It may reflect real research gaps, but it may also reflect how governance studies are framed and indexed: integrity issues such as fraud and corruption are often studied under accountability or health-financing labels, and our requirement of an explicit governance analysis may have excluded studies that address these issues without using governance terms. [Author check: reconcile with the coding rule for the heat map; see note under Table 3.] Whatever the cause, integrity and participation are dimensions where policy makers currently have the least synthesised empirical evidence to draw on.

## **4.2 Interdependence of the TAPIC dimensions**

The dimensions functioned as an interlinked system rather than as separate domains. Public reporting of performance and budgets (Transparency) underpins the oversight, audit and complaint mechanisms on which Accountability depends; the same electronic claims and audit tools appear as accountability measures and as integrity safeguards; and effective participation requires both open information and the capacity of citizens and civil society to engage. Policy Capacity underlies all the others, since independent audit, HTA, strategic purchasing and integrated information systems require skilled staff, data and management. The frequent reporting of Capacity may partly reflect its breadth, as it is the dimension that most readily encompasses other governance functions, and this should be considered when comparing counts across dimensions.

## **4.3 Contrasting challenges and good practices**

Challenges reported in Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Tanzania, India and Malawi, and in LMICs more broadly, involve opaque decision-making, weak sanctions, political interference, informal payments and shortages of technical capacity. Good practices came largely from Australia, Thailand, the Nordic countries and other high-performing systems, and centred on public disclosure, institutionalised oversight and complaint mechanisms, electronic claims with risk-based audit, systematic engagement of citizens and Indigenous communities, and mature HTA and purchasing capability. These contrasts are descriptive. The included studies mostly cannot show that a given governance practice caused better UHC performance, and practices from high-income settings may depend on resources and institutions that are not present elsewhere. The reported limited impact of social accountability mechanisms on health outcomes in South and South-East Asia is a reminder that adopting a mechanism does not guarantee results unless participants have real influence and institutions respond.

## **4.4 Implications for policy and practice**

The evidence supports several considerations for countries strengthening UHC governance, including Thailand, whose own experience appears in the evidence as an example of stakeholder consultation and public disclosure [7]:

- **Make information public by default,** including benefit-package content, provider payment methods, expenditure (with private-sector data), and performance and quality reports.
- **Pair oversight with enforceable consequences,** through independent audit, institutionalised complaint and redress channels, subnational accountability frameworks, and sanctions that are applied in practice.
- **Institutionalise participation and include marginalised groups,** for example on governing bodies and in benefit-package decisions, and build the capacity of citizens and civil society to take part.
- **Manage conflicts of interest and fraud systematically,** using legislation, electronic claims, verification and independent anti-corruption investigation with authority to act.
- **Invest in capacity,** in particular HTA, strategic purchasing, financial management at subnational levels, integrated health information systems, and change-management skills for implementation.

The 56 indicators in Table 4 can serve as a starting checklist for governance assessment, adaptable to a country’s scheme structure and to individual UHC functions.

## **4.5 Strengths and limitations**

Strengths include the use of a single, explicit governance framework applied across UHC core functions, a multi-source search including grey literature, dual screening with third-reviewer arbitration, and the derivation of an operational indicator set. Limitations should be acknowledged. Included studies were heterogeneous and mostly qualitative, and counts in the evidence map reflect the volume of evidence rather than its quality or the strength of association. [Add or amend after confirming whether critical appraisal was performed.] Three full texts could not be retrieved. The search was restricted to the listed sources and the search date, and some relevant grey literature or non-indexed national reports may have been missed. [Add language restrictions, if any.] Coding studies to TAPIC dimensions and functions involved judgement, and dimensions overlap, so classification of individual findings may differ between reviewers. Finally, evidence from single countries is concentrated in a few settings, particularly Nigeria, so findings may not generalise to Latin America, East Asia or high-income systems, which are less represented among the single-country studies.

## **4.6 Research priorities**

Future research should (i) generate empirical evidence on integrity and participation in UHC, including on conflicts-of-interest management and the effect of participation on decisions; (ii) study governance of provider contracting, budget allocation and benefit-package decisions in more depth; (iii) use quantitative and quasi-experimental designs to link governance arrangements to UHC outcomes such as coverage, financial protection and equity; (iv) test and validate the proposed indicators and assess their feasibility in different settings; and (v) examine governance across multiple schemes within the same country.

# **5. Conclusions**

Governance is central to whether UHC reforms achieve equitable, efficient and sustainable coverage, yet the evidence is skewed towards descriptive studies of capacity and accountability, with limited evidence on integrity, participation and purchasing functions. Consistent challenges include opacity, weak enforcement, political interference, exclusion of vulnerable groups and shortages of technical and information capacity, while reported good practices centre on public disclosure, independent oversight, systematic engagement and strong technical capability. The TAPIC-based indicator set gives policy makers and researchers a structured basis for assessing UHC governance, but empirical validation and outcome-linked evaluation remain priorities.
