💡 Reading this: Assessors agree with each other on individual items (0.9). But in roughly 1 case in 4 to 1 in 3, the computer's level differs from what professionals decide. The board is the safety valve, and it most often moves people up. The paper gives no kappa, no direction breakdown for the pilot, and no link to outcomes.

What the board catches that the algorithm misses

The paper's examples of upgrades come from the assessor's free-text notes:

↑
Fluctuating ability

The computer says "independent", but the assessor notes the person's abilities fluctuate.

↑
Environment

The person lives in a rural area with no grocery stores.

↑
Household risk

The person lives with a violent alcoholic spouse.

🧩
Notice the paradox

The algorithm deliberately excludes family and housing. Yet two of the three upgrade examples are exactly that: environment and household. Social context comes back in through the human review layer.

Reported successes, first 3 years

✅
Accepted

The public accepted needs certification. Early anxiety among seniors and physicians about computer assessment faded.

💴
On budget

Overall spending stayed within budget, partly because premiums were adjusted to each municipality's LTC resources.

🚪
Better access

Access improved, especially for middle- and upper-income seniors. Low-income people who used to get free care now pay 10%, but municipalities subsidise them.

🏗️
Supply responded

Providers entered the market quickly to meet demand.

🖧
National data network

Every initial and final assessment is stored centrally. This standardizes assessment and lets MHLW monitor each municipality.

The authors call this "an important shift from a health and welfare policy based on symbiotic relationships with pressure groups to one based on scientific data."
