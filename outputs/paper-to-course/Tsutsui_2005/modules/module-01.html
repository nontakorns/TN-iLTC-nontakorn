From care by family to care by society

Tsutsui & Muramatsu, J Am Geriatr Soc 2005;53:522–527. Why Japan needed a national, objective way to decide who gets long-term care.

The paper in one paragraph

In April 2000 Japan launched a mandatory LTCI scheme. Every person aged 65 and older became entitled to long-term care based strictly on physical and mental status, not income or family. To sort millions of applicants fairly, Japan built a computer-aided assessment: an 85-item questionnaire feeds a statistical model that estimates daily care minutes and assigns one of seven need levels. A local expert board then reviews each result. The paper describes how the system was built, how it works, and what went wrong in the first three years.

📄
Paper type

A descriptive health-policy report ("International Health Affairs" section), not an empirical study. The first author led the 1995 time study the model is built on, so this is an insider's account.

What was broken before 2000

🏥
Hospitals as care homes

Nursing homes and home care were scarce. Seniors with LTC needs often stayed in hospital for years, which partly explained Japan's long average hospital stays.

🧾
Means-tested welfare

LTC sat in the tax-funded welfare system, aimed at low-income seniors with little family support. It carried stigma and gave providers little reason to compete.

⚖️
Subjective eligibility

Local officials judged income and family availability case by case. Criteria differed from case to case and area to area, and the process was seen as "subjective, arbitrary, and unfair."

🏛️
Political pressure

Political pressure often got functionally independent seniors admitted to nursing facilities.

The scale problem

At launch, about 2.8 million older people were expected to apply to roughly 3,000 municipalities (the insurers). There were not enough doctors, nurses and social workers to assess everyone by professional judgment. So the core design question became:

❓
Core question

How do you decide LTC eligibility for millions of people in a way that is objective, fair, efficient, and acceptable to every citizen, without relying on scarce professionals for every case?

The abstract says "more than two million"; the body says 2.8 million. Both refer to expected applicants at launch.

Arithmetic check (Claude)

Figure 1 gives the 2004 budget as 5,451.5 billion yen, "$509 billion if $1 = 107 yen". 5,451.5 ÷ 107 ≈ $51 billion, so the dollar figure in the paper looks like a typo. Quote the yen figure.

A system borrowed from three places

🇩🇪
Germany (LTCI since 1994)

The overall social-insurance model.

🇸🇪
Scandinavia

Community-based management: municipalities are the insurers. They run the finances, set 65+ premiums, and plan and oversee services.

🇬🇧
United Kingdom

Care management. A new profession of "care managers" (licensed professionals with 5+ years' experience plus a few days' training) draws up individual care plans.

For-profit and not-for-profit providers compete in a quasi-market, and seniors choose their providers.
